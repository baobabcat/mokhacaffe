The story

One harbor, three centuries of coffee

Mokha Caffè · August 2026

Coffee does not come from Mokha. It grows on terraced mountainsides high above the Yemeni coast, in places like the Haraz range, where farmers have coaxed trees out of stone for centuries. But for roughly three hundred years, nearly every bean those farmers grew passed through one small Red Sea port before the world tasted it — and the port's name stuck to the drink. Al-Mokha is the reason café menus say mocha.

The brew before the port

By the fifteenth century, coffee drinking was established in Yemen. Sufi communities are among the earliest documented drinkers, using the brew — qahwa — to stay wakeful through night devotions. From the mosques of Sana'a it spread to the great cities of the Islamic world: Mecca, Cairo, Damascus, Istanbul. The beans themselves came from the Yemeni highlands, where coffee cultivation was already a serious agricultural enterprise.

The great entrepôt

Al-Mokha — Al-Mukhā, on Yemen's Tihama coast — rose as the trade's funnel. Highland coffee came down to the harbor by caravan; from there it crossed the Red Sea, traveled up to Suez, and moved on to Cairo, Alexandria, and the Mediterranean. When European demand exploded in the seventeenth century, the English, Dutch, and French East India companies all established trading posts in the town to secure their share. For a time, “Mokha coffee” simply meant coffee.

Yemeni merchants guarded their advantage fiercely. The usual account — repeated by generations of coffee histories — is that beans for export were boiled or otherwise rendered incapable of germination, so no one could plant their own. Whether every shipment was treated this way or not, the effect is well documented: for roughly two centuries, Arabia supplied the world's coffee.

For a time, “Mokha coffee” simply meant coffee.

The monopoly breaks

Monopolies invite smugglers. Around the turn of the eighteenth century the Dutch began cultivating coffee in Java from seedlings obtained through the Malabar coast; the French followed in the Caribbean, the Spanish and Portuguese across Latin America. Within a few decades, coffee grew on three continents and Al-Mokha's grip loosened for good. The harbor itself silted, the trade routes moved on, and the town faded to a fraction of its former self.

What survived was the name. “Mocha” remained shorthand for fine Arabian coffee — and for the distinctive, naturally wine-and-cocoa-toned cup the Yemeni highlands produce. Later, in European and American cafés, the word attached itself to the pairing of coffee with chocolate. That is the inheritance on today's menus: a drink named for a port that never saw a chocolate bean.

Why we took the name

We chose Mokha because it is a reminder of what coffee actually is: an agricultural product that travels. Behind every cup is a farm, a harvest, a road to a port, and a chain of people whose work either gets honored or hidden. A brand named for the first great coffee port has no excuse for hiding it.

So that is our standard as we build: name the origin, credit the growers, and get the history right. We are at the beginning — sourcing research, no coffee for sale yet — and we intend to build the way we have started: in the open, one documented step at a time.
