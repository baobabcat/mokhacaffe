What “mocha” really means: port, bean, drink

Mokha Caffè · 2026-08-25 · 5 min read

Ask three coffee people what “mocha” means and you may get three answers: a port in Yemen, a kind of coffee bean, or a chocolatey café drink. All three are right, and the confusion is about four hundred years old. Here is the untangling — and, at the end, a home recipe for the drink that does the name justice.

1. The port

Al-Mokha (also written Mocha, Moka, or Al-Mukhā) is a harbor town on Yemen's Red Sea coast. From the sixteenth into the eighteenth century it was the world's great coffee entrepôt: highland Yemeni coffee came down by caravan and left by ship for Egypt, Istanbul, Venice, and eventually London and Amsterdam. For a long stretch of coffee history, “Mokha” was less a place than a synonym for the drink itself. The full story is on our story page.

2. The bean

Because Yemen's exports all flowed through that one harbor, “Mocha” became the trade name for Arabian coffee — and it stuck to the cup character. Traditional Yemeni coffee is grown on mountain terraces, typically dried with the fruit still on the seed (what we'd now call a natural process), and is famous for a wild, winey, cocoa-toned profile. When a roaster today describes a coffee as “mocha” in the bean sense, they usually mean this flavor family — especially the chocolate note. Yemeni coffee remains rare and expensive, which is one reason the word migrated away from geography and toward flavor.

3. The drink

The café mocha — espresso, chocolate, steamed milk — is the youngest meaning of the three. Coffee-and-chocolate pairings were common in Europe by the eighteenth century, and as “mocha” came to signal a cocoa-toned cup, the name slid from the bean to the beverage. No port, no Yemen required: the modern mocha is named for a flavor association, not an origin.

The drink is named for a flavor the port's coffee was famous for — not for anything the port ever shipped.

Making the drink properly at home

A good mocha is a latte with real chocolate in it — not a syrup delivery system. The two failure modes are too sweet and not chocolate-forward enough. This ratio fixes both:

  • Coffee: a double espresso, or ~60 ml of very strong moka-pot coffee.
  • Chocolate: 15–20 g of dark chocolate (60–70%), finely chopped — or 10 g of good unsweetened cocoa with 5–10 g sugar.
  • Milk: 150–180 ml, steamed or heated to roughly 60–65 °C.
  1. Brew the coffee directly onto the chopped chocolate in your cup. Let it sit thirty seconds, then stir into a smooth ganache. (If using cocoa, make a paste with a splash of hot coffee first — dry cocoa clumps.)
  2. Steam or heat the milk and pour it in, holding back foam until the end if you want latte art.
  3. Taste before adding any sugar. Good dark chocolate is usually enough; the coffee should still be clearly present.

The point of the ratio is balance: chocolate first, coffee second, sweetness last. If it tastes like dessert, cut the chocolate before you cut the coffee.
