Yemeni coffee today: war, qat, and a genetic trove

Mokha Caffè · 2026-08-26 · Updated 2026-09-13 · 5 min read

The earliest credible evidence of coffee drinking comes from Yemen in the middle of the fifteenth century. Sufis used it to stay alert during night devotions. The arabica plant grew wild in Ethiopia, but Qima Coffee's research says it reached Yemen at least six hundred years ago and was probably first cultivated there. Yemen now produces a small share of the world's coffee, but its coffee genetics and highland farms remain part of the crop's story.

The mother trees

Qima Coffee reports that more than 98 percent of known cultivated arabica varieties can be traced to Yemen. Its genetics project with Dr. Christophe Montagnon surveyed more than 25,000 square kilometres and announced a previously unknown mother population on August 14, 2020. Qima calls that population Yemenia and says it is found only in Yemen. Qima and the Alliance for Coffee Excellence also assembled a jury of 35 cuppers from 24 companies in 14 countries to assess coffees from the project.

What the mountains still grow

Yemeni coffee is still grown on highland farms, often in small quantities. Qima says the country's share of world production is now below 0.1 percent. The 2026 Best of Yemen results include natural-process lots, in which the fruit dries around the seed. They also list honey, fermentation, and other processes, with jury scores ranging from 87.50 to 90.22. Flavor varies by farm, variety, processing, and roast. Our earlier article explains how the word "mocha" moved from a port to coffee and chocolate drinks.

The competition: qat

Qat, also spelled khat, competes with coffee for land and water. The World Bank figures quoted in the available economic reference estimate that qat represented 10 percent of Yemen's GDP, employed about 150,000 people, and consumed about 30 percent of irrigation water. Those figures describe an earlier period and should not be read as current measurements. The same source says coffee production declined as qat cultivation increased.

The war

The civil war that began in 2015 has damaged Yemen's economy and infrastructure. Mokha was bombed in July 2015. A missile and drone attack in 2021 damaged warehouses at the port. Coffee farming and trade continue under those conditions, but this article does not attempt to track current routes or control of the port.

The revival

Several organized projects now document and sell Yemeni specialty coffee:

  • Best of Yemen. The Alliance for Coffee Excellence and Qima held the seventh Best of Yemen auction on September 10, 2026. Its published results list 33 lots.
  • Yemenia. Qima's Yemenia page documents the mother population announced in 2020 and says the company intends to continue the research.
  • Yemeni Coffee Day. Yemen marks March 3 with a national festival intended to encourage coffee cultivation.

These projects do not resolve the water shortage or the war. The sources reviewed here also do not establish that the economics of Yemeni coffee have improved across the country.

What it means in the cup

A country name does not guarantee a flavor or tell you how a purchase benefits a farmer. Read the farm, variety, process, lot size, and sourcing details. The published 2026 auction results show how wide the descriptions can be, including flowers, stone fruit, berries, chocolate, spices, and fermented notes. They also identify each lot, its weight, winning bid, and buyer.

Yemen's place in coffee history is only one part of the story. Current farms and lots need current, specific reporting.
