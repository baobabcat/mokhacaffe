Yemeni coffee today: war, qat, and a genetic trove

Mokha Caffè · 2026-08-26 · 6 min read

Yemen is where the world learned to drink coffee. The earliest credible evidence of coffee drinking — Sufi devotees in Yemen's monasteries, brewing to stay alert through night devotions — dates to the mid-fifteenth century, and the terraced highlands above the Red Sea were likely the first place the Ethiopian forest shrub was ever farmed as a crop. Six centuries later, the first coffee country produces only a sliver of world supply. This is the story of what happened in between — and why the specialty-coffee world is watching Yemen again.

The mother trees

Arabica is Ethiopian by birth, Yemeni by adoption. The plant traveled from Ethiopia's forests to Yemen at least six hundred years ago, and there — on arid mountain terraces, under centuries of domestication and natural selection — it became something new. Those Yemeni trees went on to seed most of the arabica the world grows today: according to Qima Coffee's genetics program, more than 98% of known cultivated arabica varieties trace back to Yemeni stock. In August 2020, Qima and geneticist Christophe Montagnon announced the result of the largest genetic survey in Yemen's history: a previously unknown mother population of arabica, found only in Yemen, which they named Yemenia. To gauge its quality, Qima and the Alliance for Coffee Excellence convened an independent jury — 35 cuppers from 24 companies across 14 countries — which ranked the coffees among the highest in the world. The first coffee country, it turns out, is still holding undiscovered genetics.

What the mountains still grow

The farming has changed less than the world around it. Yemeni growers still work stone terraces cut into highland slopes, still pick ripe cherry by hand, still dry it whole with the fruit on — the natural process the rest of the coffee world later rediscovered as a specialty technique. The cup is the profile that made “mocha” a flavor word: winey, cocoa-toned, wild in the best sense (we untangled the word's three meanings in an earlier post). Quantities are small and famously variable — lots are counted in bags, not containers — which is part of why genuine Yemeni coffee commands prices far above commodity grades.

The competition: qat

Yemeni coffee's most successful rival is not another coffee. It is qat (also spelled khat), a mildly narcotic leaf chewed socially across the country. Qat pays farmers faster and more reliably than coffee, and it drinks: the World Bank has estimated qat cultivation at around a tenth of Yemen's GDP, employing some 150,000 people while consuming roughly 30 percent of the country's irrigation water — this in one of the most water-scarce countries on earth. Every terrace switched from coffee to qat is a double loss: the plants and the water do not come back quickly. Coffee's decline as Yemen's flagship export tracks qat's rise almost exactly.

The war

Since 2015, civil war has been layered over all of it. Mokha itself — the port our brand is named for — was bombed in 2015, and in 2021 a missile-and-drone attack wrecked warehouses at its harbor. Exports now route around damaged ports and checkpoints; farmers harvest anyway. The human scale of that effort reached a wide audience through Dave Eggers' 2018 bestseller The Monk of Mokha, the true story of Mokhtar Alkhanshali, a Yemeni-American from San Francisco who built a supply chain of Yemeni smallholder lots and finally escaped the country by boat with his coffee — the story behind his company, Port of Mokha.

The revival

The attention is coming back, and this time it is organized:

  • Best of Yemen. The Alliance for Coffee Excellence has run a Best of Yemen auction every year since 2022, with a 2026 edition upcoming — putting Yemeni micro-lots directly in front of roasters worldwide.
  • Yemenia. Qima's genetics work continues to map a mother population that could matter for arabica's future far beyond Yemen.
  • Yemeni Coffee Day. Yemen now marks March 3 as a national coffee festival to encourage cultivation — an official push to pull terraces back from qat.

None of this fixes the water, and none of it ends the war. But for the first time in generations, the economics of Yemeni coffee point up instead of down.

What it means in the cup

When a roaster offers a Yemeni lot, you are looking at the rarest intersection in coffee: the original cultivated genetics, six hundred years of terrace farming, a war economy, and a crop counted in bags. Expect intensity — dried fruit, cocoa, wine-like acidity — and expect to pay for it. Chosen carefully, it is also the most meaningful purchase in specialty coffee: the money flows back to the place coffee began.

Yemen taught the world to drink coffee. The least the coffee world can do is remember where it learned.
