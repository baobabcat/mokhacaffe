Coffee bean types: what you are actually choosing

Mokha Caffè · 2026-09-09 · 7 min read

A bag can describe its coffee as arabica, Bourbon, Ethiopian, natural process, and light roast. Those labels are not five competing bean types. They describe different layers of the same coffee: its species, plant variety, origin, processing, and roast.

The short answer. Nearly all commercially grown coffee is arabica or robusta. Liberica and excelsa are much less common. For buying coffee, species is only the first clue. Roast, processing, freshness, and how you brew have just as much influence on the cup.

Species, variety, origin, and roast are different

Most "types of coffee beans" lists mix together several categories. Keeping them separate makes labels easier to read.

  • Species is the broad botanical group, such as Coffea arabica or Coffea canephora, commonly called robusta.
  • Variety is a genetic line within a species. Typica, Bourbon, Caturra, and Gesha are examples associated with arabica.
  • Origin tells you where the coffee grew. Country alone is broad; a named region, cooperative, farm, or lot is more specific.
  • Process describes what happened to the coffee fruit after picking, such as washed, natural, honey, or wet hulled.
  • Roast describes how the green seed was transformed by heat. Light, medium, and dark are not plant types.

None of these labels guarantees quality. They are clues that become useful when you connect them to the flavors you notice.

Arabica and robusta account for almost all commercial coffee

The National Coffee Association reports that arabica and robusta represent more than 99 percent of coffee grown for commercial use. It estimates arabica at about 62 percent of global production and robusta at about 38 percent.

Arabica

Arabica is usually the species behind specialty single-origin coffee. Its beans tend to contain less caffeine than robusta. The plant favors milder conditions and is more vulnerable to disease, which can make it harder and more expensive to grow.

Flavor varies too much for one promise, but arabica is often chosen when sweetness, acidity, aroma, and origin character matter. A dark roast can still mute those distinctions, while poor brewing can flatten an excellent lot.

Robusta

Robusta grows in warmer conditions and generally resists disease better than arabica. Its beans contain roughly 50 to 60 percent more caffeine, according to the National Coffee Association. It often brings a stronger, more bitter profile and is common in instant coffee and espresso blends.

Robusta is not automatically bad coffee. Careful farming and processing can produce high-quality robusta, and some espresso drinkers want the body and intensity it can add. Judge the producer and the cup, not just the species name.

What about liberica and excelsa?

Liberica and excelsa appear in far smaller quantities. The National Coffee Association lists both among the less common coffees while putting arabica and robusta at more than 99 percent of commercial production. Scarcity can make a coffee interesting, but rare does not mean better.

Consumer guides do not always use plant names consistently. Read the roaster's description of the coffee and where it was grown rather than buying from a dramatic "four bean types" claim alone.

A variety is not another species

World Coffee Research maintains separate catalogs for arabica and robusta varieties. It notes that much of cultivated arabica descends from Typica and Bourbon, followed by rust-resistant introgressed varieties and newer F1 hybrids.

Variety can influence plant health, yield, and cup character, but it works together with soil, climate, elevation, harvest, processing, roasting, and brewing. Gesha does not guarantee floral coffee. Bourbon does not guarantee sweetness. A variety name is useful evidence, not a tasting result.

Choose beans by the cup and brewer you want

Start with the drink you make most often, then use the label to narrow the choice.

  1. Choose a flavor direction. Chocolate, nut, and caramel notes usually point toward a familiar, round cup. Fruit, citrus, or floral notes suggest a brighter profile.
  2. Match the roast to that direction. Medium and medium-dark roasts often suit people who want lower perceived acidity and more roast flavor. Lighter roasts preserve more of the coffee's origin and processing character.
  3. Consider the brewer. Filter and pour-over methods can make subtle differences easier to notice. Espresso and moka pot coffee often benefit from beans that remain balanced at high strength. French press suits people who enjoy more body.
  4. Check freshness and bag size. Buy an amount you can use while it still tastes lively. A roast date is more informative than a vague premium label.
  5. Change one variable next time. Keep the brewer and recipe steady while trying a different roast, origin, process, or species. That is how the label starts to mean something to your palate.

A simple first purchase

If you have no preference yet, choose a recently roasted arabica or arabica-forward blend with a medium roast and familiar tasting notes. Brew it with a measured recipe. Our coffee ratio calculator gives you a repeatable starting point.

For your second bag, change one thing. Try a lighter roast from the same roaster, compare a washed and natural coffee, or choose a blend that includes robusta. Avoid changing the beans, grinder setting, water, and ratio at once. You will know that the cups differ, but not why.

Read the rest of the bag

Species and variety occupy only two lines on a detailed label. Roast date, farm or region, elevation, process, tasting notes, and certification claims need their own context.
