How to read a coffee bag

Mokha Caffè · 2026-09-09 · 5 min read

Specialty coffee bags can read like field reports: roast date, country, farm, variety, altitude, process, roast level, and a string of tasting notes. Some details help you choose a coffee. Others need context. This guide explains what each line can tell you, and what it cannot.

Start with the roast date

A roast date tells you when the coffee was roasted. A best-by date tells you how long the producer expects an unopened package to retain acceptable quality. They answer different questions, so one does not make the other useless.

Coffee loses aroma after roasting as oxygen, heat, moisture, and light take their toll. There is no single perfect age for every bean, roast, package, or brewing method. The National Coffee Association suggests buying enough freshly roasted coffee for one or two weeks, storing it airtight in a cool, dark place, and grinding only what you need just before brewing. Old coffee is usually stale rather than unsafe unless moisture has allowed mold to grow.

Species and variety

Arabica accounts for about 60% of world coffee production and is the usual species in specialty coffee. Robusta, properly Coffea canephora, generally has more caffeine and a more bitter taste. It is common in espresso blends, and its presence does not by itself make a coffee poor.

A variety name, such as Bourbon or Typica, identifies the plant's genetic line. It can be useful, but it does not predict the cup on its own. Farm conditions, picking, processing, roasting, and brewing still matter. A peaberry is a round, single seed that develops in place of the usual pair of flat-sided seeds. Peaberries make up roughly 10 to 15 percent of coffee beans. Sellers sometimes charge more for them, though the label alone does not establish better quality.

Altitude

Altitude is often listed in metres above sea level, sometimes shortened to MASL. Arabica is widely grown in highland conditions, but elevation is a rough clue rather than a quality score. Latitude, temperature, rainfall, shade, variety, and farm practices all change what a given number means. A specific farm or elevation range can show that the roaster knows where the lot came from. A missing number does not prove poor quality or poor traceability.

Process: what happened after picking

The process line describes how producers removed and dried the fruit around the coffee seed. It can shape flavor, but the familiar taste descriptions below are tendencies, not promises.

  • Washed. Producers remove the skin and most of the fruit before drying. Traditional wet processing uses fermentation and washing, although mechanical methods can use less water. Washed coffees are often described as clean or clear in flavor.
  • Natural. The whole cherry dries before the fruit is hulled away. Drying can take several weeks. Natural coffees are often described as fruity or sweet, but careful drying matters because excess moisture can cause defects. The dried husk is also used for qishr.
  • Honey or pulped natural. Producers remove the skin but leave some sticky mucilage around the seed while it dries. The name refers to that texture, not to added honey.
  • Wet hulled. In Indonesia's giling basah method, the parchment is removed while the bean is still wet. These coffees are often described as low in acidity and heavy in body.

Roast level and caffeine

Terms such as city, French, and Italian are not a universal scale. They describe how far the roast developed, but one roaster's medium can look like another's dark. Lighter roasting usually leaves more of the flavors associated with the green coffee and its processing. As roasting continues, flavors created by heat become more prominent, and oils may appear on the bean surface.

Dark roast does not automatically contain more caffeine. Caffeine is fairly stable at normal roasting temperatures, while the beans lose water and expand. How you measure the dose matters: by volume, denser light-roast beans may supply more coffee mass than darker beans; by weight, the difference from roast level is small.

Certifications and sourcing claims

Check who stands behind a seal. Fairtrade International's standards cover social, economic, and environmental requirements, a minimum price and premium system, and independent audits by FLOCERT. A Fairtrade mark refers to that system. It is not a general synonym for every claim written as "fair trade."

In the United States, a packaged product labeled "organic" must generally be certified and contain at least 95 percent organic ingredients, excluding salt and water. USDA says labels must be approved by an accredited certifier. Organic certification concerns how a product was produced and handled. It is not a score for flavor.

Direct trade has no single definition, common standard, or universal certifier. Treat it as the roaster's account of a sourcing relationship. Useful evidence includes named producers, prices or premiums, repeat purchases, and a public sourcing report.

Tasting notes

"Apricot, bergamot, brown sugar" is usually a sensory description, not an ingredient list. Nothing was necessarily added. Read tasting notes as a rough map: citrus and floral terms suggest a different cup from chocolate and nut terms. Your water, grinder, recipe, and palate may produce a different result.

Putting it together

Start with five questions:

  1. When was it roasted, and is the bag sized for how quickly you drink it?
  2. Where did it come from: country, region, cooperative, farm, or lot?
  3. What species or variety is named?
  4. Was it washed, natural, honey processed, or wet hulled?
  5. Does the roast level and tasting-note family match what you enjoy?

Details do not guarantee a good cup. They do let you compare coffees, repeat purchases you liked, and ask better questions when a label is vague.
