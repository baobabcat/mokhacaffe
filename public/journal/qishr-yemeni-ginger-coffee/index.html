Qishr: Yemen's ginger coffee, brewed from the husk

Mokha Caffè · 2026-08-28 · 5 min read

Ask what coffee is made of and the answer seems obvious: the bean. But the bean is only the seed. Around it sits a sweet red fruit — the coffee cherry — and for most of coffee's history in Yemen, the part people actually brewed was not the seed at all. It was the husk. The drink is called qishr (also spelled gishr or kishr, from the Arabic word for “skin” or “husk”): a hot infusion of dried coffee-cherry skins, ginger, and sometimes cinnamon. It is still poured across Yemen today, and it may be older than the roasted-bean coffee the rest of the world drinks.

Older than the roast

Coffee crossed the Red Sea from Ethiopia into the Arabian Peninsula, where Sufi dervishes began cultivating the shrub — the same monastic origin story we covered in our post on Yemeni coffee today. But roasting beans and grinding them for brewing was a later refinement. The early drinks used the fruit itself: Yemenis first made a beverage from the pulp of the coffee berries, and that drink — used in religious ceremonies — was known as qishr. On present evidence, an infusion of the coffee cherry was likely drunk in Yemen before the form of coffee we know today. In much of Yemen it never fully gave way: qishr is still the everyday alternative to coffee there, partly for a deeply practical reason — husks do not need roasting. No fuel, no roast fire, no grinder. Dry fruit, hot water, done.

What the travelers found

The outsider accounts are striking. Harry St. John Philby, crossing the Arabian highlands in the first half of the twentieth century, recorded the region's hospitality in his 1952 book Arabian Highlands: “Great hospitality was shown to us on entering their houses; we were always pressed to stay, and never allowed to go without taking a cup of coffee or rather an infusion of coffee husk called 'keshr'; for, strange to say, though in the heart of the coffee country, coffee is never taken as a beverage.” In the Tihama foothills he found the same pattern — “Ginger coffee is the universal drink and the cup is always filled, a guest being given two cups at once… Qishr, an infusion made from the husks of coffee berries, is also drunk.” In the heartland of the crop that taught the world to drink coffee, the welcome cup was made from the part everyone else threw away.

Why Yemen has husks to spare

Yemeni coffee is almost entirely natural-processed: the cherries are dried whole, fruit and all, often on rooftops, before the dried husk is cracked away from the bean. That means every harvest generates exactly the raw material qishr needs — sun-dried husk — as a byproduct of the same ancient process that produces the beans. Nothing wasted; two drinks from one tree.

The modern echo: cascara

The rest of the coffee world spent centuries treating the cherry as mill waste — then rediscovered it. Coffee-cherry tea is now sold as cascara (Spanish for “husk” or “peel”), a name popularized by Salvadoran producer Aida Batlle, who noticed the discarded husks' floral aroma around 2005 and began drying them for brewing; by 2009 cascara was being served in US coffee shops. Demand from large American chains has since pushed prices for dried husks, in some cases, above the price of the beans themselves — a strange fate for a byproduct Yemen had been brewing for centuries. One caution for home brewers: despite the word “tea,” the husk is a coffee-plant product and the infusion does contain caffeine. Treat it like coffee, not like a bedtime herbal.

Brewing qishr at home

Recipes vary by household — this is a starting point, not canon. You need dried coffee husks, sold today as cascara (check a specialty roaster or tea merchant); everything else is ordinary kitchen stock.

  • Ratio. About 3 tablespoons of dried husks per 300–350 ml (10–12 oz) of water — the standard cascara proportion.
  • Steep or simmer. For a cascara-style cup, just off the boil for about 4 minutes, then strain. For the Yemeni style, simmer the husks gently for 5–10 minutes instead.
  • Spice. Add a few slices of fresh ginger while it brews — this is the heart of qishr. A small piece of cinnamon stick is the common optional second spice.
  • Serve. Hot, in small cups, sweetened to taste; in Yemen the cup is refilled without being asked. Expect a light, tea-like body with notes of dried fruit, hibiscus, and honey — more like a fruit tisane than like black coffee.

Cold version: the same husks steep well in cold water for 12–16 hours in the fridge — smoother and sweeter, good over ice.

The world throws away the husk and keeps the seed. Yemen kept both — and may have started with the part the rest of us discard.
